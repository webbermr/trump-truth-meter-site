Claims checked

  1. True
    “About 125 million people voted in the first round of Brazil’s presidential election.”
    Brazil’s electoral court reported about 125.1–125.3 million ballots out of roughly 158.7 million registered voters, a turnout near 79%. Rounding to 125 million is fair. “Today” is slightly off for a post timestamped early Monday GMT: voting was Sunday, October 4, 2026, in Brazil. [1][2][3]
  2. Mostly true
    “Brazil’s results came out shortly after the vote closed, on the same day.”
    Polls closed at 5 p.m. Brasília time and electronic totals began almost immediately. The runoff between Flávio Bolsonaro and Lula was clear that evening. Full national totalization finished in the early hours of Monday after a brief data stall, so “shortly after” and a complete same-calendar-day close are slightly overstated, but same-night results are how the system works. [2][3][4][5]
  3. Misleading
    “In Detroit, Philadelphia, California, and other U.S. cities and states, results with much smaller numbers often take weeks to calculate.”
    Official certification can take weeks by law—about three weeks in Pennsylvania, roughly 20–25 days in Michigan, and up to about 30–38 days in California—and California often counts a large share of mail ballots for days or weeks after Election Day. Unofficial results and media calls are usually much faster: in 2024, Pennsylvania and Michigan reported the bulk of the presidential vote within hours, not weeks. Detroit and Philadelphia are far smaller than Brazil’s national electorate; California cast about 16 million ballots in 2024, and the U.S. as a whole about 155 million, more than Brazil. The comparison also ignores Brazil’s single national electronic system versus U.S. paper and mail ballots processed by separate local offices. [6][7][8][9]
  4. False
    “Those slower counts are used to rig elections, and voting in America is corrupt.”
    The post offers no evidence of rigging. Election officials attribute the time to mail-ballot processing, postmark deadlines, signature checks, provisional ballots, canvasses, and audits. A slower official count is not evidence of fraud. The “Rig, I mean, calculate” line and “CORRUPT” are accusations, not shown facts. [6][7][8]
