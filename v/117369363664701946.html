Claims checked

  1. Mostly true
    “The bill Trump calls the “No More Changing of Clocks Act” is the Sunshine Protection Act, which would end twice-yearly clock changes by making daylight saving time permanent (“leave it as it is now”).”
    H.R. 139, the Sunshine Protection Act, would make daylight saving time the permanent standard and stop the twice-yearly switch. “No More Changing of Clocks Act” is Trump’s nickname, not the official title. In early October the U.S. is still on daylight time, so “leave it as it is now” matches what the bill would do. [1][2]
  2. True
    “The House passed the bill 308–117 on July 14, 2026, with broad bipartisan support, and it is now before the Senate.”
    The House clerk records Roll Call 238 on July 14, 2026, as 308 yeas and 117 nays (193 Republicans, 114 Democrats, and 1 independent voting yes). The bill was received in the Senate the next day. Support was bipartisan but not unanimous. [3][4]
  3. Misleading
    “The bill is being held up in the Senate by Senator Tom Cotton of Arkansas for reasons unknown.”
    Cotton has repeatedly opposed permanent daylight saving time, including a public objection to fast-tracking a version of the bill, citing dark winter mornings and school safety. Those reasons are not unknown. The 2026 House-passed bill was referred to committee; the record does not show that Cotton alone is the only obstacle. [5][6][4]
  4. False
    “Brian Rolapp is the PGA commissioner and needs the bill so winter golf tournaments can be completed.”
    As of October 2026, Rolapp is CEO of PGA Tour, Inc.; his term as commissioner is scheduled to begin January 1, 2027. He is not “PGA Commissioner.” No public statement was found in which he said this law is needed to finish winter tournaments. [7][8]
  5. Mostly true
    “Far and away, most people prefer an extra hour of evening sunlight rather than morning light.”
    A February 2026 YouGov poll found 64% wanted to stop changing clocks and 64% preferred that it get dark later in the evening. That is a clear majority, not “far and away” unanimity, and other polls show the public splits once the choice is permanent daylight time versus permanent standard time. [9][10]
  6. False
    “Nobody wants to change clocks twice a year; the idea has a 100% approval rating.”
    In the same YouGov poll, 16% opposed ending the clock changes and others were unsure. Medical groups and some senators actively oppose this bill. “100%” and “nobody” are false. [9][11]
  7. Misleading
    “Even most farmers would rather have additional evening time to harvest their crops.”
    The American Farm Bureau Federation has supported making daylight saving time permanent, citing consistency and rural productivity. That is not the same as evidence that most individual farmers want extra evening light specifically to harvest. Historically, many farm interests opposed daylight saving time because morning chores depend on early light. [12][13]
  8. Unverifiable
    “Changing clocks costs the country a fortune, and cities and states with watchtowers and hard-to-reach clocks spend millions of dollars.”
    Economic estimates of the time switch vary widely and are not a settled national “fortune.” No credible accounting was found that ties millions in public spending specifically to adjusting watchtowers and remote clocks twice a year.
  9. Misleading
    “Leading studies have shown conclusively that permanent extra evening daylight would decrease robbery and murder, cut car and pedestrian crashes, lower cardiac, stroke, and seasonal-depression risk, make children’s walks home safer, and save money on energy bills.”
    The spring clock change is linked to short-term rises in heart attacks and crashes, and some research ties more evening light to fewer robberies. But sleep-medicine groups say permanent daylight time is worse for health than permanent standard time; crash effects differ by time of day and road user; energy savings from extended daylight time are small or, in some studies, negative; and none of this is conclusive for murder, stroke, depression, school safety, or household bills. Clocks also do not create an extra hour of daylight. [11][14][15][16][17]
