Claims checked

  1. Misleading
    “Polls have always underestimated MAGA, and pollsters try to suppress the vote.”
    National polls underestimated Trump in 2016, 2020, and 2024. That is not true of every cycle, including some midterms, and it does not show that pollsters intend to suppress voting. [1]
  2. Mostly true
    “Trump won all seven swing states, the popular vote, and 86% of counties in 2024.”
    He carried the seven battlegrounds and won the popular vote with about 49.8%, a plurality. Independent county tallies put his share at roughly 85–86%, so 86% is a fair rounding of an unofficial count. [2][3][4]
    🔁 Checked 3 times before.
  3. Misleading
    “Trump won 99% of primaries this year.”
    In context this means his endorsed candidates, not Trump himself on a primary ballot. Ballotpedia put the 2026 Trump-endorsement win rate at about 97% in July; later losses left it in the mid-to-high 90s, not 99%. His own Oct. 1 graphic said 98.37%. [5][6][7]
  4. Misleading
    “Trump is on the ballot in 2026.”
    He is not a candidate in the Nov. 3, 2026 midterms. “I am on the ballot” is a slogan he and his pollster have used to mean voters should treat the midterms as a referendum on him, including “pretend I’m on the ballot.” [8][9]
  5. Misleading
    “Since November 2025, monthly McLaughlin polls have kept Trump’s job approval between 47% and 50%, and the latest poll this month is 49%, the same as his 49.8% vote share.”
    McLaughlin’s published monthly series from late 2025 through September 2026 sits around 48–50%. The latest release found, taken Sept. 16–22, is 48% approve, not 49%, and no October McLaughlin national survey was located. 49% is also not “the exact same” as 49.8%. That series is a Republican pollster’s outlier: late-September averages and Reuters/Ipsos put approval in the low-to-mid 30s. [2][10][11][12]
  6. Opinion
    “No other political figure has ever had this level of rating consistency.”
    Dick Morris’s “never in fifty years” line is a personal judgment about one pollster’s series, not a checkable record.
  7. Misleading
    “The same McLaughlin poll shows a 50–48 Democratic generic ballot, and a Democratic lead under four points historically means Republicans keep the House.”
    McLaughlin’s September survey shows Democrats ahead 48–43 on the generic ballot, not 50–48. The four-point cutoff is a rule of thumb, not a law: in 2020 Democrats won the House national vote by about three points and kept the majority. [10][13][14]
  8. False
    “Almost all polls are showing Republican gains on the generic ballot.”
    Late-September averages had Democrats ahead by about 7 to 9 points, and tracked averages moved further toward Democrats during September, not toward Republicans. [15][16][17]
