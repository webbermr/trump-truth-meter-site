Claims checked

  1. Mostly true
    “The national debt is about $40 trillion and heading toward $50 trillion.”
    Gross federal debt crossed $40 trillion in August 2026 and was about $40.1 trillion in late September. A rise to $50 trillion is a projection, not a reported fact.
  2. Mostly true
    “Trustees say Social Security goes broke in six years and Medicare in seven.”
    The 2026 trustees project the OASI trust fund depleted in late 2032 and the Medicare hospital fund in 2033. "Go broke" overstates it: payroll taxes would still cover a large share of scheduled benefits after depletion.
  3. True
    “Not a single Republican voted for Obamacare.”
    Final passage of the ACA was party-line, with no Republican yes votes in the Senate in December 2009 or the House in March 2010.
  4. False
    “Democrats raised health-care costs under Obama by expanding Obamacare and calling it Medicare for All.”
    The ACA and later expansions were never branded Medicare for All. That name belongs to a separate single-payer proposal that was not enacted.
  5. True
    “From 1993 to 2023, the share of women ages 25–34 with a four-year degree rose from 23% to 47%, and men from 24% to 38%.”
    Those match Pew Research figures on bachelor's-degree attainment. Levin slightly mislabeled them as "attending" college.
  6. False
    “Sugar and meat were still rationed in the United States in the early 1950s.”
    U.S. meat rationing ended in 1945 and sugar rationing in 1947. Rationing into the 1950s was a British, not American, experience.
  7. Mostly true
    “Adjusted for inflation, U.S. gasoline cost more in 1981, 2008, and 2022 than in spring 2026, with a real June 2008 peak around $6.20.”
    Levin is reading Randal Olson's June 2026 analysis, and the direction is right: those earlier peaks were higher in real terms than spring 2026. Exact real-dollar peaks vary with the CPI base and whether the measure is a weekly peak or an average.
  8. False
    “California has a 72-cent-per-gallon sales tax on gasoline, the highest in the nation.”
    California's state gasoline excise tax was 61.2 cents through June 30, 2026, and 63.4 cents after July 1—the highest state excise rate—not a 72-cent sales tax. Sales tax is a separate percentage that varies by location.
  9. Misleading
    “Gasoline and energy prices are higher because of Democrats, not Trump or the Iran war.”
    Prices did rise sharply in 2021–22 under Biden. In 2026, though, the national average climbed from the high-$2 range early in the year into the mid-$4 range after the Iran war disrupted oil flows, which the EIA treats as the main driver of that year's spike. State taxes can add to the pump price, especially in California, but they do not explain the national jump.
  10. Mostly true
    “As of June 2026 the inflation-adjusted U.S. home-price index was 170.2, up from 169 in May and 3.8% below the May 2022 peak of 176.9.”
    Those figures match a CPI-deflated Case-Shiller national index rebased to January 2000 = 100. Real prices were still near the 2022 peak, which undercuts the monologue's claim that housing strain is mostly lifestyle rather than price.
  11. Misleading
    “Young adults' affordability problems have little to do with government or the economic system and are mainly lifestyle decisions.”
    College debt, dining out, and travel do affect some budgets, but they do not explain away record-high real home prices relative to income or the 2026 energy shock.
