Claims checked

  1. Unverifiable
    “A table of distinguished people just told Trump they hate going home in the dark immediately after a mandatory time change, and one said he hates going home in darkness.”
    This is a private conversation he reports. No public record confirms or contradicts who said it.
  2. Mostly true
    “Immediately after the mandatory time change, people go home at night and it is dark.”
    Under current federal law, clocks fall back at 2 a.m. on the first Sunday in November—November 1, 2026—which shifts sunset about an hour earlier and makes late-afternoon and evening commutes darker. The spring change does the opposite, and Hawaii and most of Arizona do not change clocks. On the day of the post, October 3, that fall change had not yet happened. [1][2]
  3. Opinion
    “It is depressing to go home in the dark after the time change.”
    Whether darker evenings feel depressing is a personal reaction, not a checkable fact.
  4. Misleading
    “In crime-ridden blue states, it is a lot more dangerous to walk home in the dark.”
    Darkness does raise some risks: most U.S. pedestrian deaths occur at night, and research finds street robbery and some other offenses more likely after dark. That is not a blue-state effect. Recent FBI-based rankings put several Republican-leaning states (Alaska, Arkansas, Louisiana, Tennessee) among the highest violent-crime states, alongside New Mexico; several of the lowest-crime states are Democratic. Party label is a weak predictor next to urbanization and local conditions. [3][4][5][6]
