The House did pass the Sunshine Protection Act 308–117, and the bill would lock in permanent daylight saving time. Trump overstates Cotton as the sole unexplained obstacle, invents near-unanimous public support, and inflates his latest Arkansas margin.

Claims checked

  1. Mostly true
    “The Sunshine Protection Act would end twice-yearly clock changes by making current daylight saving time permanent.”
    H.R. 139, the Sunshine Protection Act, would make daylight saving time the year-round standard so clocks would not spring forward or fall back. Trump’s alternate title, “We Don’t Want to Change Our Clocks Twice a Year Act,” is his nickname, not the bill’s name. Permanent DST does not create extra daylight; it shifts an hour of winter light from morning to evening. [1][2]
    🔁 Checked once before.
  2. True
    “The House passed the Sunshine Protection Act by a vote of 308 to 117.”
    On July 14, 2026, the House passed H.R. 139 by 308–117, with votes from both parties. [1][3]
    🔁 Checked once before.
  3. Misleading
    “Senator Tom Cotton is holding up the bill in the Senate for some unknown reason, and one senator can stop it.”
    Cotton has publicly opposed permanent daylight saving time, citing darker winter mornings and risks to schoolchildren, and a single objection can block a unanimous-consent path. His reasons are not unknown, one senator is not an absolute veto if leadership schedules a cloture vote, and reporting does not establish he is the only senator who objects. [4][5][6]
    🔁 Rated misleading once before (Oct 2).
  4. False
    “Almost everybody wants the Sunshine Protection Act except Tom Cotton.”
    The House vote included 117 no votes. Sleep researchers, some farm groups, and other lawmakers prefer permanent standard time or oppose locking in daylight saving time. Public opinion is split between permanent DST and permanent standard time. [3][5][7][8]
  5. Mostly true
    “Trump won Arkansas three times, most recently by 34 points.”
    He carried Arkansas in 2016, 2020, and 2024. Official 2024 results were about 64.2% to 33.6%, a margin of roughly 30.6 points, not 34. [9]
  6. False
    “65% or more want permanent afternoon daylight (daylight saving time as it is now), and 100% do not want to change clocks twice a year.”
    Large majorities dislike switching clocks, but not 100%. Recent polls put support for ending the switch around the mid-60s at best, with a clear minority who want to keep changing clocks. Preference for permanent DST versus permanent standard time is split and well short of 65% consensus for permanent DST in several major surveys. [7][10][11]
    🔁 Rated false once before (Oct 2).
  7. Misleading
    “Brian Rolapp, CEO of the PGA Tour, wrote in support of the bill because the Tour needs more afternoon daylight to finish tournaments.”
    Rolapp is CEO of the PGA Tour, not the PGA of America commissioner, and the attached Oct. 1, 2026 letter does endorse the Sunshine Protection Act, citing evening recreational play, broadcast windows, and industry estimates. It does not say Tour events, including winter tournaments, cannot be finished without the bill. [12][13]
    🔁 Rated false once before (Oct 2).
  8. Misleading
    “Permanent DST would add 23 million golf rounds and $1 billion a year (about $250,000 per facility); afternoon and evening tee times generate about 40% more revenue than morning play; more than one million Americans work in the golf economy.”
    Those figures are National Golf Course Owners Association advocacy estimates repeated in the Rolapp letter, not independent government measurements. Broader golf-economy employment is often cited near or above one million when indirect jobs are included, but the rounds and revenue numbers are projections. [14][15]
  9. Unverifiable
    “Changing clocks twice a year is very expensive because of high and dangerous steeples on churches and municipal buildings.”
    Clock changes have documented costs in scheduling, sleep, and traffic accidents. There is no credible evidence that resetting steeple or tower clocks is a major national expense or a central reason for the bill. [16]
  10. Unverifiable
    “479-453-0533 is Senator Tom Cotton’s cellphone.”
    Cotton’s official Senate contact page lists office numbers, not this number as his personal cellphone. The number has circulated in political posts, but that does not independently confirm it. [17]
